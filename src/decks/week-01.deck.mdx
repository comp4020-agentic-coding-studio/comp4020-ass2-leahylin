## Three questions, before I say anything

1. What is in your fridge right now that you will **throw away**?
2. When did you last **smell** something to decide whether to eat it?
3. What did your grandparents do in **February** that you do not do?

```notes
**~6 min** (0:03)

Hands up, then talk to the person beside you for three minutes. This is the
first thing that happens in this course, and it is deliberate: the evidence
base for the semester is their own kitchens, and I want that established in
minute three rather than week four.

Take three or four answers out loud. Do not correct anybody. Write the
answers on the board and leave them there for the whole two hours — I come
back to them at the end.

Typical answers, in my experience:
- Q1: half a bag of spinach, something in a takeaway container, condiments
  bought for one recipe. Almost nobody says "nothing."
- Q2: a lot of hesitation. Some say they never do. Some say they smell milk
  and nothing else. This is the competence question arriving early, and it
  arrives from them rather than from me, which is much better.
- Q3: usually "preserving," "gardening," or a blank. The blank is the
  interesting answer.

Do not let this run past six minutes. It will want to.
```

## What this course argues

The refrigerator is not a natural necessity.

It is a specific **historical**, **technological** and **gendered**
construction — and it can be read the way you would read any other
institution.

Twelve weeks, one argument, four movements.

```notes
**~3 min** (0:09)

Now the framing. Keep it fast; there is a whole semester to elaborate.

The word to lean on is **construction**. Not "bad," not "unnecessary" —
constructed. Something was built, by people, for reasons, and those reasons
are recoverable.

Flag the three adjectives because they map onto the modules: historical
(weeks 1–3), gendered (week 2 in particular), technological (throughout).
```

---

## The four movements

**Weeks 1–3 · Disenchantment**
Cold as a manufactured commodity. The myth sold alongside it.

**Weeks 4–6 · Micropolitics**
The interior as a jurisdiction. The door as a stage.

**Weeks 7–9 · The tyranny of time**
Date labels, waste, and the stuffed fridge as solace. Where it becomes

**Weeks 10–12 · Resistance and reconstruction**
Surveillance in the kitchen, degrowth, and what could be built instead.

```notes
**~3 min** (0:12)

Point at the shape, not the content. What matters is that they see it

The one sentence to say out loud: **this course does not reset every
```

---

## What this course is not

Not a **design** course. We are not redesigning the fridge.

Not a **sustainability** course. "Waste less" is a conclusion, not a method.

Not a course about **your** moral failure. Nobody here throws food away
because they are careless.

```notes
**~2 min** (0:15)

Say this early and mean it. Every year somebody arrives expecting either an
engineering brief or a guilt exercise, and both expectations poison the
fieldwork — one produces gadget proposals, the other produces confessional
essays that mistake shame for analysis.

The line that does the work: **if a system reliably produces the same
outcome across millions of households, that outcome is a property of the
system, not of the households.** Hold onto it for week 8, where they will
measure their own waste and immediately want to apologise for it.
```

# Part one

## Coexisting with decay

```notes
**~1 min** (0:17)
```

## For most of human history

People did not **fight** decay.

They **coexisted** with it.

```notes
**~2 min** (0:18)

The pivot of the whole first hour, so land it slowly.

The framing to resist — and they will all arrive with it — is that
pre-refrigeration food practice was a set of *workarounds*: what people did
because they could not yet do the real thing. That reading makes the fridge
the destination and everything before it a draft.

It is the wrong reading, and the next four slides are why.
```

---

## The techniques

- **fermentation** — controlled microbial transformation
- **drying** — removing the water that decay needs
- **salting and curing** — osmotic pressure, applied patiently
- **potting and confit** — sealing under fat
- **cellaring** — cool and humid, not cold and dry

Each one is a different negotiation with time. None of them is refrigeration
attempted badly.

```notes
**~5 min** (0:20)

Work across the list and give one concrete example each. Ask the room for
them first — somebody's family makes kimchi, somebody's cures meat, somebody
grew up with a root cellar. Their examples beat mine.

- fermentation: kimchi, sauerkraut, yoghurt, cheese, miso, soy sauce, bread,
  beer, wine. Note how much of what we call cuisine is on this list.
- drying: sun-dried tomatoes, jerky, stockfish, dried mushrooms, pasta.
- salting: bacalhau, prosciutto, bacon. Salt cod fed European navies and
  built transatlantic trade routes.
- potting: confit de canard, potted shrimp.
- cellaring: apples, potatoes, carrots in sand, cabbage. Cool but *not*
  cold — around 4–10 °C and humid, which a modern fridge is actively bad at.

The last bullet is the argument. Say it twice.
```

{/* _class: centered */}

## Nobody invented cheese by trying to keep milk cold.

```notes
**~2 min** (0:25)

This is the sentence they will remember from week 1, so give it silence
either side.

The point underneath the joke: fermentation is not a way of *stopping*
change. It is a way of *directing* it. The milk still goes off. It goes off
in a direction somebody wanted.

Then extend it: cheese, wine, bread, yoghurt, soy sauce, vinegar, cured
meat. A very large fraction of what any culture considers its finest food is
decay that somebody steered. Refrigeration cannot produce any of it. It can
only slow the clock on food that already exists.

If you want one more beat: the fridge is a **pause button**. Fermentation is
a **composer**.
```

---

## What those techniques required

**Knowledge** — which microbes, which salt, which temperature, how long.

**Judgement** — is this ready, is this turning, is this gone.

**A calendar** — the work happened when the season said, not when you felt
like it.

**A household organised around all three.**

```notes
**~5 min** (0:27)

This slide is the one the course is actually built on, so do not rush it.

Each of the four is a *capacity*, and capacities can be lost. That is the
thread running to week 7: when the judgement in bullet two is outsourced to
a number printed on a jar by a manufacturer's legal department, something
has been transferred, and it was not transferred to the household.

The fourth bullet is the gendered one, and it is worth naming now even
though week 2 does the work. That household organisation was labour, it was
skilled, it was largely women's, and it was almost entirely unwaged and
unrecorded. When we say a technology "saved labour," the first question is
always: **whose, and where did it go?**

Ask: what happens to a skill when the thing that required it disappears?
Expect "you lose it." Push once: lose it *how* — individually, or across a
generation? Nobody in this room chose to stop knowing this. It was not
taken from them personally. It stopped being transmitted.
```

## Decay as an actor, not an enemy

Under the **old** arrangement, decay was:

- **predictable** — it followed the season
- **legible** — you could see, smell and taste where you were in it
- **partly useful** — some of it you wanted

```notes
**~2 min** (0:33)

Establish the old column on its own before showing the new one — the
contrast lands much harder as a reveal than as a table they can read ahead
on.

All three are properties that made decay *manageable by a person*. Nothing
here required a machine. It required attention, and attention was
distributed across a household that expected to be paying it.
```

---

## And under the new arrangement

- **deferred** — pushed out of sight, not removed
- **illegible** — hidden behind a closed opaque door
- **entirely a loss** — there is no good version of it any more

```notes
**~4 min** (0:35)

Now set them side by side out loud, pair by pair.

The word to dwell on is **deferred**. The fridge does not abolish decay. It
buys time on it, and the debt is still owed. That is the thesis in
mechanical form, and everything in week 8 is the invoice arriving.

"Illegible" is the sneaky one. A root cellar is *open* — you walk past it,
you see the apples, the ones going soft are visible and you eat those first.
A fridge is a closed opaque box in which the oldest item is at the back and
by construction cannot be seen. Ask the room how many have found something
unidentifiable at the back of theirs. Every hand goes up. That is not a
personal failing, it is an architectural property of the object, and week 4
takes it apart properly.

Third pair: this is where the good version of decay disappears. In a
fermenting household, some transformation is the product. In a refrigerating
household, every transformation is a loss. We narrowed the range of
acceptable outcomes to exactly one — *unchanged* — and then built a machine
to enforce it.
```

## Cold as a commodity

## The nineteenth century's strangest business

Sell a substance that:

- falls **from the sky**
- for **free**
- and **destroys itself** in transit

```notes
**~3 min** (0:39)

Pose it as a business-school problem before naming anybody. Ask the room how
they would do it. Somebody always says "sell it somewhere hot," which is
exactly right and exactly the point.

The reason this framing matters: it makes visible that a commodity is not a
natural category. Ice was not a commodity, and then it was, and the
difference was work — legal, logistical, promotional work — done by
identifiable people for identifiable reasons.

Everything in this course is a version of that observation.
```

---

## Frederic Tudor, the "Ice King"

Boston, **1806**. Cuts a pond, loads a brig, ships ice to **Martinique**.

Loses a great deal of money.

Does it again. And again.

```notes
**~4 min** (0:42)

The detail worth having: the first voyage was the brig *Favorite*, roughly
130 tons of ice, February 1806. It was a commercial failure, and the failure
was not melt — a surprising amount arrived.

The failure was that **nobody in Martinique wanted ice.** They had never had
it. There was no ice-shaped hole in their lives, no icehouse to store it in,
no bar that knew what to do with it.

This is the single most useful fact in the lecture, so make it explicit:
Tudor's real problem was not supply. It was **demand**, and demand had to
be manufactured.

His method — and Rees is good on this — was to give it away first. Cold
drinks free to bartenders until customers began asking for them, then
charge, having created the taste. He also negotiated monopoly supply rights
in the ports he entered.

Ask: what does it tell you about a need, that it had to be taught?
```

---

## Making the trade work

**1825** — Nathaniel Wyeth's horse-drawn ice cutter. Harvest stops being
hand-sawn and becomes industrial.

**Sawdust** as insulation — a waste product of the timber mills, and the
thing that made long voyages survivable.

**1833** — the *Tuscany* sails from Boston to **Calcutta**. Roughly a third
of the cargo melts. It is still a triumph.

```notes
**~4 min** (0:46)

Three innovations, none of them refrigeration.

Wyeth's plough is the mechanisation of *harvest* — a grid scored into the
pond, uniform blocks, horses instead of hands. Cost per ton collapses. Note
that the technology arrived on the supply side long before anything cooled
anything artificially.

Sawdust is my favourite detail, because it is an industrial by-product
becoming the enabling condition for a different industry. Nothing about it
was designed for ice.

Calcutta: the *Tuscany*, 1833, roughly 180 tons loaded and something like
100 tons landed. The British community there built an icehouse for it. New
England pond water, sold at a profit, on the other side of the planet, in a
sailing ship, before refrigeration existed. If a student ever tells you
globalisation began with container shipping, this is the counter-example.
```

---

## Who paid for it

Ice cutting was **seasonal**, **freezing**, **dangerous** work.

Men and horses went through the ice. Ice houses collapsed.

The trade had routes, owners, insurers, futures, and a workforce — like any
other extractive industry.

```notes
**~3 min** (0:50)

Do not skip this slide for time. It is the one that makes the phrase
"political economy" mean something concrete rather than decorative.

Cold arrived in a glass in Calcutta because somebody stood on a frozen pond
in Massachusetts in January with a saw. That labour is invisible in the
product — which is a general property of commodities and the reason this
course keeps insisting on following the chain backwards.

Tie forward: in week 10 the same move gets made with electricity and data.
The cold in a modern fridge also comes from somewhere, and somebody is also
standing in it.
```

---

## And then it ended

Warm winters. Polluted ponds. **Typhoid** traced to natural ice.

And a competitor that did not depend on weather at all.

```notes
**~3 min** (0:53)

The natural ice trade did not lose on price alone. It lost on *reliability*
and on *hygiene* — the same industrial pollution that fouled the water
supply fouled the ponds, and by the late nineteenth century ice from a river
downstream of a city was a plausible disease vector.

The structural point, and it is the bridge to part three: manufactured cold
wins because it is **indifferent to conditions**. It does not care whether
the winter was mild. It does not care where you are. Take the weather out of
the supply chain and the supply chain can be planned.

Planning is the whole prize. Everything after this is about what a household
does once its food supply becomes plannable.
```

# Part three

## Manufacture replaces harvest

```notes
**~1 min** (1:06)

Restart crisply. One sentence of recap: cold was harvested, shipped and
sold; now it gets built on site.
```

---

## Mechanical refrigeration comes first to industry

Breweries. Meatpacking. Cold storage warehouses. Ships.

Decades before it comes to a kitchen.

The technology was not invented for the home. It was **retrofitted** to it.

```notes
**~4 min** (1:07)

The chronology matters and students consistently get it backwards. Vapour
compression is a nineteenth-century industrial technology — Carré, Linde and
others — and its first customers were businesses whose product spoiled at
scale.

Two consequences worth stating:

First, refrigerated shipping restructured world agriculture before it
restructured any household. Refrigerated meat over long distances meant
Australia and Argentina could sell to Britain. Whole national economies
reorganised around a cooling technology while domestic kitchens still had an
insulated box and a delivery of pond ice.

Second — and this is the one to hold — **the domestic fridge is an
industrial machine that was shrunk and sold into a house.** It arrives
carrying industrial assumptions: continuous operation, standardised
temperature, throughput. It was never designed around what a household
actually does with food. Week 4 is essentially this observation, extended.
```

---

## The problem with early refrigerants

**Ammonia. Sulfur dioxide. Methyl chloride.**

Efficient. Well understood. And they leaked.

In a factory a leak is an industrial accident. In a kitchen it is a family.

```notes
**~4 min** (1:11)

Here is the obstacle that had to be cleared before the fridge could become
domestic, and it was not cost and it was not size.

Early domestic units used refrigerants that were variously toxic,
asphyxiating or flammable. Leaks in the 1920s killed people in their homes.
There was real and rational public wariness about putting one of these
machines in a kitchen.

So the barrier to the domestic refrigerator was a **safety** problem, and
the thing that solved it was a **chemistry** problem, solved by a
corporation, for a market. Keep that sequence — the object in their kitchen
has a specific corporate research programme in its ancestry, and the next
slide names it.
```

---

## Freon, 1928

Thomas Midgley Jr., working for **General Motors** and **Frigidaire**.

Dichlorodifluoromethane. Non-toxic. Non-flammable. Stable.

Demonstrated by inhaling a lungful and using it to blow out a candle.

Manufactured by a **DuPont–GM joint venture**.

```notes
**~4 min** (1:15)

The candle demonstration — at an American Chemical Society meeting, 1930 —
is worth telling properly, because it is theatre and it worked. One breath
proves non-toxic, one exhalation extinguishing a flame proves
non-flammable. The safety case, made in about four seconds.

The three adjectives are the whole product: non-toxic, non-flammable,
stable. Stable is the one that matters later and nobody noticed at the time.

Name the corporate structure deliberately — GM, Frigidaire, DuPont, and
Kinetic Chemicals as the joint venture that made it. This is not incidental
colour. The domestic refrigerator became possible because a chemical was
developed to make a product line viable. The engineering served the market,
in that order.
```

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> \[Midgley] had more impact on the atmosphere than any other single
> organism in Earth's history.
>
> <cite>J. R. McNeill, *Something New Under the Sun* (2000)</cite>

```notes
**~4 min** (1:19)

Midgley's other major contribution was tetraethyl lead in petrol.

CFCs turned out to be so stable that they survive intact into the
stratosphere, where UV breaks them up and the released chlorine catalyses
ozone destruction. Molina and Rowland published that in 1974; the Montreal
Protocol followed in 1987 and is, genuinely, the most successful
environmental treaty we have.

The moral to *avoid* is "scientist bad." Midgley was solving the exact
problem he was asked to solve, and he solved it well. Nobody in 1928 had a
framework in which "chemically inert" was a hazard.

The moral to actually draw: **the property that made it safe indoors is the
property that made it catastrophic outdoors.** Stability at kitchen scale
is persistence at planetary scale. A domestic virtue became an atmospheric
vice without changing at all — only the boundary of the analysis moved.

That is a methodological instruction for this whole course. Where you draw
the edge of the system determines what counts as a success.
```

---

## What the compressor actually changed

Before: cold is **harvested** somewhere cold, **shipped**, and **runs out**.

After: cold is **manufactured**, **on site**, **on demand**, **indefinitely**.

Cold stops being a delivery and becomes a **utility**.

```notes
**~3 min** (1:23)

Slow down on the last line — it is the hinge of the second hour.

Delivery: finite, visible, arrives on a schedule, you can see it diminish in
the box. Every ice delivery is a reminder that cold is a thing that costs
something.

Utility: continuous, invisible, metered, and it disappears into a bill that
arrives monthly with the lighting and the kettle. Nobody can tell you what
fraction of their power bill is refrigeration. Ask. Nobody will know.

The ice man was an interface. The compressor removed it, and with it the
last moment at which a household had to think about cold as a purchase.
```

## What "season" used to mean

A **constraint** on what could be eaten.

A **calendar** for when work happened.

A **shared** condition — the whole community was in the same month.

```notes
**~3 min** (1:29)

Season as infrastructure, not aesthetics. It scheduled labour, it structured
festivals, it determined which techniques you used and when.

The third bullet is easy to miss and worth stating: seasonality was
*collective*. Everyone around you was eating roughly the same thing at
roughly the same time, because everyone faced the same constraint. Food
practice was synchronised by the weather rather than by choice.

Australian framing lands well here: ask what is in season in Canberra in
February, then ask what is in the supermarket in February. The list in the
second answer does not vary across the year, and that flatness is the
achievement being described.
```

---

## What it means now

A **label**. A **marketing category**. A **preference**.

Strawberries in July. Asparagus whenever.

The constraint is gone. The **cold chain** replaced it: field, packhouse,
truck, warehouse, supermarket, car, your fridge.

```notes
**~4 min** (1:32)

July in this hemisphere is midwinter. The strawberries are there anyway.

Walk the cold chain out loud, slowly, and count the links. Every one is
refrigerated, every one is powered, every one is owned by somebody, and the
last one — the only one they pay for directly and the only one they can
see — is the box in their kitchen.

The point to extract: their fridge is not a standalone appliance. It is the
**final metre of a planetary logistics system**, and the household has been
quietly enrolled as its last-mile operator. They pay for that link. They
maintain it. They absorb its failures as personal waste.

Nobody signed anything.
```

---

## Time became negotiable

If cold is purchasable, then **time is purchasable**.

Buy in bulk. Cook on Sunday for Thursday. Keep the option open.

Every one of those is a small, sensible, individually rational decision.

```notes
**~4 min** (1:36)

Be genuinely even-handed here, because the argument is stronger for it and
because the room will stop trusting a lecture that only prosecutes.

These are real gains. Bulk buying is cheaper and matters most to the people
with the least money. Batch cooking buys time back for people working
multiple jobs. Leftovers kept safely are food not wasted. A fridge is a
significant public health technology — safe milk, safe meat, safe insulin.
None of that is in dispute and I want it on the record in week 1.

The claim is not that the fridge is bad. It is that the fridge sells a
particular thing — *deferral* — and that the price of deferral is not
visible at the point of sale.

Then the setup for week 9: what does "keep the option open" cost, if the
option is never exercised? Hold that question. It is the whole back half of
the course.
```

## What we mean by political economy

Not economics. Not politics.

The study of **who gets what**, and of the **arrangements of power and
property** that decide it.

Usually applied to states, industries, classes.

Here: to **one appliance, in one kitchen.**

```notes
**~4 min** (1:41)

Define the term properly once, now, because it is in the course title and
they will use it for twelve weeks.

The unusual move is the scale. Political economy at the scale of a nation is
ordinary. At the scale of a household appliance it sounds like a category
error, and part of the intellectual work of this course is showing that it
is not.

Why it is not: the fridge is where a very large number of systems terminate
in a small number of decisions. Agricultural policy, energy generation,
retail concentration, food safety regulation, advertising, gendered domestic
labour, municipal waste — all of it converges on one person at 11pm deciding
whether the leftovers are still all right.

**The fridge is where a global system becomes a personal decision.** That is
why it is worth a semester, and it is the best answer to give when somebody
at a party asks what the course is about.
```

---

## Reading one object

For anything in a kitchen, ask:

1. Who **built** it, and what were they selling?
2. What **capacity** did it replace, and where did that go?
3. What does it make **visible**? What does it **hide**?
4. Who **pays** when it fails — and in what currency?

```notes
**~5 min** (1:45)

This is the course's method on one slide. Tell them to photograph it.

Run the fridge through all four out loud so they see the machine work:

1. GM, DuPont, Frigidaire, Kelvinator — selling appliances, and from week 2,
   selling modernity itself with the appliance attached.
2. Preservation skill, seasonal knowledge, sensory judgement. It went
   nowhere. It stopped being transmitted, which is not the same as being
   destroyed and is harder to reverse.
3. Visible: the front of the shelf, the door, the performance of a stocked
   household (week 6). Hidden: the back of the shelf, the energy draw, the
   supply chain, the eventual bin.
4. The household pays, in money, in wasted food, in the unpaid labour of
   managing the box — and the planet pays in emissions and methane. Not the
   manufacturer, and not the retailer whose bulk pricing encouraged the
   over-purchase.

Then invite them to run it on something else — a freezer, a microwave, a
supermarket loyalty card, a meal-kit subscription. Take one from the floor
and do it live. It works on all of them, and watching it work on an object
they chose is what convinces people the method is real.
```

{/* _class: quote */}

> The fridge sells you time,
> and the bill arrives as waste.

```notes
**~3 min** (1:50)

The thesis. Everything so far has been the setup for this sentence and they
should feel that.

Take it in halves.

"Sells you time" — deferral, optionality, the postponement of a decision
about food. That is the product. It is a real product and people buy it
rationally.

"The bill arrives as waste" — deferral is not cancellation. The decision
still has to be made, later, with worse information, about food that is now
older, behind other food, in an opaque box. Most of the time it gets made by
default, and the default is the bin.

Say plainly: this is a **claim**, not a fact. It is falsifiable and I intend
to spend eleven weeks trying to break it in front of them.
```

## What would prove me wrong

The claim fails if:

- households with fridges waste **less** food than comparable households
  without, controlling for income
- the lost competence turns out **not** to be lost — people can still judge
  food without a label
- the time bought is actually **used**, rather than banked and forgotten

Week 8 measures the first. Week 7 tests the second. Week 9 asks the third.

```notes
**~5 min** (1:54)

This is the most important slide in the deck for how the course gets
assessed, and it is the one students remember in their essays.

A thesis that cannot fail is not a thesis, it is a mood. I want them
adversarial from week 1, and I want them to see that the falsification
conditions are already scheduled into the syllabus rather than being
something I hope nobody asks about.
```

## Wednesday: the blind tasting

Three samples of the same vegetable. Unlabelled.

**A** — picked fresh   **B** — refrigerated three days   **C** —
freeze-dried and rehydrated

You taste all three **without knowing which is which**, and write a
description of each **before** the reveal.

```notes
**~4 min** (2:02)

Non-negotiable logistics, so say all of it:

- **Do not skip this one.** It cannot be repeated once you know what the
  samples are, and it is one of only two exercises in the course that cannot
  be made up later.
- **Email me by Monday** about any allergy or dietary requirement. The
  samples get adjusted; nobody sits out the first tutorial.
- Read the Rees excerpts first. Nothing else to prepare — materials are
  supplied, and they have to be, because the whole thing depends on you not
  knowing.
- **Describe, do not guess.** Texture, moisture, smell, colour, taste, in
  your own words. The instinct is to try to identify which is which. Resist
  it; that is not the exercise and it wrecks the data.
- **Keep your three descriptions.** They are your own baseline and people
  use them in the Critical Essay every year.

What reliably happens, though do not tell them in advance: the room agrees
strongly about texture and disagrees completely about which sample is
"freshest," and several people find they preferred one they would have
rejected if it had carried a label.
```
