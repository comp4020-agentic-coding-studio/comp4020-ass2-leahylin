## The techniques

<div className="def-rows">
  <div><b>Fermentation</b><p>Controlled microbial transformation</p></div>
  <div><b>Drying</b><p>Removing the water that decay needs</p></div>
  <div><b>Salting and curing</b><p>Osmotic pressure, applied patiently</p></div>
  <div><b>Potting and confit</b><p>Sealing under fat</p></div>
  <div><b>Cellaring</b><p>Cool and humid, not cold and dry</p></div>
</div>

<p style={{ fontSize: "1.5rem", color: "#000" }}>Each one is a different negotiation with time. <strong>None of them is refrigeration attempted badly.</strong></p>

```notes
**~5 min** (0:20)

Work across the list and give one concrete example each. Ask the room for
them first — somebody's family makes kimchi, somebody's cures meat, somebody
grew up with a root cellar. Their examples beat mine.

- fermentation: kimchi, sauerkraut, yoghurt, cheese, miso, soy sauce, bread,
  beer, wine. Note how much of what we call cuisine is on this list.
- drying: sun-dried tomatoes, jerky, stockfish, dried mushrooms, pasta.
- salting: bacalhau, prosciutto, bacon. Salt cod fed European navies and
  built transatlantic trade routes.
- potting: confit de canard, potted shrimp.
- cellaring: apples, potatoes, carrots in sand, cabbage. Cool but *not*
  cold — around 4–10 °C and humid, which a modern fridge is actively bad at.

The last bullet is the argument. Say it twice.
```

{/* _class: big-lines xl */}

<h2>Nobody invented cheese by trying to keep milk cold.</h2>

```notes
**~2 min** (0:25)

This is the sentence they will remember from week 1, so give it silence
either side.

The point underneath the joke: fermentation is not a way of *stopping*
change. It is a way of *directing* it. The milk still goes off. It goes off
in a direction somebody wanted.

Then extend it: cheese, wine, bread, yoghurt, soy sauce, vinegar, cured
meat. A very large fraction of what any culture considers its finest food is
decay that somebody steered. Refrigeration cannot produce any of it. It can
only slow the clock on food that already exists.

If you want one more beat: the fridge is a **pause button**. Fermentation is
a **composer**.
```

<div className="rule-cards" style={{ "--cols": 2 }}>

```notes
Each of the four is a *capacity*, and capacities can be lost. That is the

and where did it go?**

Expect "you lose it." Push once: lose it *how* — individually, or across a
generation? Nobody in this room chose to stop knowing this. It stopped being
```

## Decay as an actor, then decay as a loss

<div className="compare">
  <div>
    <p className="compare-label">The old arrangement</p>
    <b>Predictable</b><p>It followed the season.</p>
    <b>Legible</b><p>You could see, smell and taste where you were in it.</p>
    <b>Partly useful</b><p>Some of it you wanted.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <p className="compare-label">The new arrangement</p>
    <b>Deferred</b><p>Pushed out of sight, not removed.</p>
    <b>Illegible</b><p>Hidden behind a closed opaque door.</p>
    <b>Entirely a loss</b><p>There is no good version of it any more.</p>
  </div>
</div>

```notes
**~6 min** (0:33)

Two slides' worth of material on one, so work it as a reveal: cover the
right column, establish the old one on its own, then set them side by side
out loud, pair by pair.

The word to dwell on is **deferred**. The fridge does not abolish decay. It
buys time on it, and the debt is still owed. That is the thesis in
mechanical form, and everything in week 8 is the invoice arriving.

"Illegible" is the sneaky one. A root cellar is *open* — you walk past it,
you see the apples, the ones going soft are visible and you eat those first.
A fridge is a closed opaque box in which the oldest item is at the back and
by construction cannot be seen. Ask the room how many have found something
unidentifiable at the back of theirs. Every hand goes up. That is not a
personal failing, it is an architectural property of the object, and week 4
takes it apart properly.

Third pair: this is where the good version of decay disappears. In a
fermenting household, some transformation is the product. In a refrigerating
household, every transformation is a loss. We narrowed the range of
acceptable outcomes to exactly one — *unchanged* — and then built a machine
to enforce it.
```

## The nineteenth century’s strangest business

Sell a substance that:

  <h2>Frederic Tudor, the “Ice King”</h2>
  <div className="figure-inline">
    <b>1806</b>
    <span>Boston to Martinique,<br />in the brig <em>Favorite</em></span>
  </div>
  <p>Cuts a pond, loads a brig, ships ice to the Caribbean. Loses a great deal of money.</p>

</div>

```notes
**~4 min** (0:42)

The detail worth having: the first voyage was the brig *Favorite*, roughly
130 tons of ice, February 1806. It was a commercial failure, and the failure
was not melt — a surprising amount arrived.

The failure was that **nobody in Martinique wanted ice.** They had never had
it. There was no ice-shaped hole in their lives, no icehouse to store it in,
no bar that knew what to do with it.

This is the single most useful fact in the lecture, so make it explicit:
Tudor's real problem was not supply. It was **demand**, and demand had to
be manufactured.

His method — and Rees is good on this — was to give it away first. Cold
drinks free to bartenders until customers began asking for them, then
charge, having created the taste. He also negotiated monopoly supply rights
in the ports he entered.

Ask: what does it tell you about a need, that it had to be taught?
```

## Making the trade work

<div className="timeline">
  <div><i></i><em>1825</em><b>Wyeth’s horse-drawn ice cutter</b><p>Harvest stops being hand-sawn and becomes industrial.</p></div>
  <div><i></i><em>Sawdust</em><b>Insulation, for free</b><p>A waste product of the timber mills, and the thing that made long voyages survivable.</p></div>
  <div><i></i><em>1833</em><b>Boston to Calcutta</b><p>The <em>Tuscany</em> sails. Roughly a third of the cargo melts. It is still a triumph.</p></div>
</div>

```notes
**~4 min** (0:46)

Three innovations, none of them refrigeration.

Wyeth's plough is the mechanisation of *harvest* — a grid scored into the
pond, uniform blocks, horses instead of hands. Cost per ton collapses. Note
that the technology arrived on the supply side long before anything cooled
anything artificially.

Sawdust is my favourite detail, because it is an industrial by-product
becoming the enabling condition for a different industry. Nothing about it
was designed for ice.

Calcutta: the *Tuscany*, 1833, roughly 180 tons loaded and something like
100 tons landed. The British community there built an icehouse for it. New
England pond water, sold at a profit, on the other side of the planet, in a
sailing ship, before refrigeration existed. If a student ever tells you
globalisation began with container shipping, this is the counter-example.
```

## And then it ended

<div className="term-row">
  <span>Warm winters</span>
  <span>Polluted ponds</span>
  <span>Typhoid traced to natural ice</span>
</div>

<p className="statement">And a competitor that did not depend on weather at all.</p>

```notes
**~3 min** (0:53)

The natural ice trade did not lose on price alone. It lost on *reliability*
and on *hygiene* — the same industrial pollution that fouled the water
supply fouled the ponds, and by the late nineteenth century ice from a river
downstream of a city was a plausible disease vector.

The structural point, and it is the bridge to part three: manufactured cold
wins because it is **indifferent to conditions**. It does not care whether
the winter was mild. It does not care where you are. Take the weather out of
the supply chain and the supply chain can be planned.

Planning is the whole prize. Everything after this is about what a household
does once its food supply becomes plannable.
```

## Mechanical refrigeration comes first to industry

<div className="chain plain" style={{ "--cols": 4 }}>
  <span>Breweries</span>
  <span>Meatpacking</span>
  <span>Cold storage</span>
  <span>Ships</span>
</div>

Decades before it comes to a kitchen.

<p className="statement">The technology was not invented for the home. It was <strong>retrofitted</strong> to it.</p>

```notes
**~4 min** (1:07)

The chronology matters and students consistently get it backwards. Vapour
compression is a nineteenth-century industrial technology — Carré, Linde and
others — and its first customers were businesses whose product spoiled at
scale.

Two consequences worth stating:

First, refrigerated shipping restructured world agriculture before it
restructured any household. Refrigerated meat over long distances meant
Australia and Argentina could sell to Britain. Whole national economies
reorganised around a cooling technology while domestic kitchens still had an
insulated box and a delivery of pond ice.

Second — and this is the one to hold — **the domestic fridge is an
industrial machine that was shrunk and sold into a house.** It arrives
carrying industrial assumptions: continuous operation, standardised
temperature, throughput. It was never designed around what a household
actually does with food. Week 4 is essentially this observation, extended.
```

## The problem with early refrigerants

<div className="rule-cards">
  <div><b style={{ fontSize: "2.125rem" }}>Ammonia</b><p>Toxic</p></div>
  <div><b style={{ fontSize: "2.125rem" }}>Sulfur dioxide</b><p>Asphyxiating</p></div>
  <div><b style={{ fontSize: "2.125rem" }}>Methyl chloride</b><p>Flammable</p></div>
</div>

<p style={{ fontSize: "1.6875rem" }}>Efficient. Well understood. And they leaked.</p>

<p style={{ fontSize: "2rem", color: "#000" }}>In a factory a leak is an industrial accident. In a kitchen it is a family.</p>

```notes
**~4 min** (1:11)

Here is the obstacle that had to be cleared before the fridge could become
domestic, and it was not cost and it was not size.

Early domestic units used refrigerants that were variously toxic,
asphyxiating or flammable. Leaks in the 1920s killed people in their homes.
There was real and rational public wariness about putting one of these
machines in a kitchen.

So the barrier to the domestic refrigerator was a **safety** problem, and
the thing that solved it was a **chemistry** problem, solved by a
corporation, for a market. Keep that sequence — the object in their kitchen
has a specific corporate research programme in its ancestry, and the next
slide names it.
```

```notes
The candle demonstration — at an American Chemical Society meeting, 1930 —
```

> \[Midgley] had more impact on the atmosphere than any other single
> organism in Earth's history.
>
> <cite>J. R. McNeill, [*Something New Under the Sun*](https://archive.org/details/somethingnewunde0000mcne) (2000)</cite>

```notes
**~4 min** (1:19)

Midgley's other major contribution was tetraethyl lead in petrol.

CFCs turned out to be so stable that they survive intact into the
stratosphere, where UV breaks them up and the released chlorine catalyses
ozone destruction. Molina and Rowland published that in 1974; the Montreal
Protocol followed in 1987 and is, genuinely, the most successful
environmental treaty we have.

The moral to *avoid* is "scientist bad." Midgley was solving the exact
problem he was asked to solve, and he solved it well. Nobody in 1928 had a
framework in which "chemically inert" was a hazard.

The moral to actually draw: **the property that made it safe indoors is the
property that made it catastrophic outdoors.** Stability at kitchen scale
is persistence at planetary scale. A domestic virtue became an atmospheric
vice without changing at all — only the boundary of the analysis moved.

That is a methodological instruction for this whole course. Where you draw
the edge of the system determines what counts as a success.
```

<p className="statement">Cold stops being a delivery and becomes a <strong>utility</strong>.</p>

```notes
**~3 min** (1:23)

Slow down on the last line — it is the hinge of the second hour.

Delivery: finite, visible, arrives on a schedule, you can see it diminish in

something.

Utility: continuous, invisible, metered, and it disappears into a bill that
arrives monthly with the lighting and the kettle. Nobody can tell you what
fraction of their power bill is refrigeration. Ask. Nobody will know.

The ice man was an interface. The compressor removed it, and with it the
last moment at which a household had to think about cold as a purchase.
```

## What “season” used to mean

<div className="rule-cards">
  <div><b style={{ fontSize: "2rem" }}>A constraint</b><p>On what could be eaten.</p></div>
  <div><b style={{ fontSize: "2rem" }}>A calendar</b><p>For when work happened.</p></div>
  <div><b style={{ fontSize: "2rem" }}>A shared condition</b><p>The whole community was in the same month.</p></div>

```notes
**~3 min** (1:29)

Season as infrastructure, not aesthetics. It scheduled labour, it structured
festivals, it determined which techniques you used and when.

The third one is easy to miss and worth stating: seasonality was
*collective*. Everyone around you was eating roughly the same thing at
roughly the same time, because everyone faced the same constraint. Food
practice was synchronised by the weather rather than by choice.

Australian framing lands well here: ask what is in season in Canberra in
February, then ask what is in the supermarket in February. The list in the
second answer does not vary across the year, and that flatness is the
achievement being described.
```

## Time became negotiable

If cold is purchasable, then **time is purchasable**.

<div className="term-row">
  <span>Buy in bulk</span>
  <span>Cook on Sunday for Thursday</span>
  <span>Keep the option open</span>

```notes
**~4 min** (1:36)

particular thing — *deferral* — and that the price of deferral is not
visible at the point of sale.

option is never exercised? Hold that question. It is the whole back half of
```

<div className="columns wide-left">
  <div>
    <p>Not economics. Not politics.</p>

```notes
**~4 min** (1:41)

Define the term properly once, now, because it is in the course title and
they will use it for twelve weeks.

The unusual move is the scale. Political economy at the scale of a nation is
ordinary. At the scale of a household appliance it sounds like a category
error, and part of the intellectual work of this course is showing that it
is not.

Why it is not: the fridge is where a very large number of systems terminate
in a small number of decisions. Agricultural policy, energy generation,
retail concentration, food safety regulation, advertising, gendered domestic
labour, municipal waste — all of it converges on one person at 11pm deciding
whether the leftovers are still all right.

**The fridge is where a global system becomes a personal decision.** That is
why it is worth a semester, and it is the best answer to give when somebody
at a party asks what the course is about.
```

## Reading one object

For anything in a kitchen, ask:

<div className="num-grid">
  <div><b>1</b><p>Who <strong>built</strong> it, and what were they selling?</p></div>
  <div><b>2</b><p>What <strong>capacity</strong> did it replace, and where did that go?</p></div>
  <div><b>3</b><p>What does it make <strong>visible</strong>? What does it <strong>hide</strong>?</p></div>
  <div><b>4</b><p>Who <strong>pays</strong> when it fails — and in what currency?</p></div>
</div>

```notes
**~5 min** (1:45)

This is the course's method on one slide. Tell them to photograph it.

Run the fridge through all four out loud so they see the machine work:

1. GM, DuPont, Frigidaire, Kelvinator — selling appliances, and from week 2,
   selling modernity itself with the appliance attached.
2. Preservation skill, seasonal knowledge, sensory judgement. It went
   nowhere. It stopped being transmitted, which is not the same as being
   destroyed and is harder to reverse.
3. Visible: the front of the shelf, the door, the performance of a stocked
   household (week 6). Hidden: the back of the shelf, the energy draw, the
   supply chain, the eventual bin.
4. The household pays, in money, in wasted food, in the unpaid labour of
   managing the box — and the planet pays in emissions and methane. Not the
   manufacturer, and not the retailer whose bulk pricing encouraged the
   over-purchase.

Then invite them to run it on something else — a freezer, a microwave, a
supermarket loyalty card, a meal-kit subscription. Take one from the floor
and do it live. It works on all of them, and watching it work on an object
they chose is what convinces people the method is real.
```
