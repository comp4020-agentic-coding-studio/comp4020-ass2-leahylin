## Module 1 · Disenchantment

**1** Cold stopped being a season and became a purchase.

**2** The appliance was sold as modernity, and raised the standard of domestic
labour rather than lowering it.

**3** The kitchen became the last terminal of a global supply chain, and
locality was erased.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:02)

Four recap slides, one per module, fast. Name the claim, not the content —
they sat through the content.

Module 1's job was disenchantment, and the test of whether it worked is
whether the object still looks obvious. Week 1: cold as commodity, ice trade
to Freon, a skill becoming a subscription. Week 2: the second shift, and
technology raising the standard instead of freeing the time. Week 3: the
fridge as the last metre of a planetary logistics system.

Do not stop for questions in this section. Say so if you need to.
```

## Module 2 · Micropolitics

**4** The interior is a **jurisdiction** — someone holds the right to
refrigerate.

**5** Leftovers are classified, and the classification is about disgust and
frugality, not nutrition.

**6** The door is a **stage**, performing a household that the interior
contradicts.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:03)

The interpretive module, and the one students most often undervalue at the
time and most often use in the essay.

Week 4: who decides what enters, what gets thrown out, whose food is
displaced — a politics of a small space with real winners. Week 5: the
semiotics of the container at the back. Week 6: Goffman, front stage and
back stage, six centimetres apart.

The line worth restating: the door says who we are, the interior says what we
actually did.
```

## Module 3 · The tyranny of time

**7** The date label is a fact about **liability**, not about food.

**8** Households with reliable refrigeration waste **more** food, not less.

**9** The fridge's real product is **reassurance**, which is why the waste is
tolerated.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:04)

The measurable module, and the spine of the whole argument. If they remember
three claims from this course, these are the three.

Week 8 is the load-bearing one. Say plainly that it is the empirical claim
the whole thesis rests on, that it is counter-intuitive, and that it
survived the semester's scrutiny — including their own waste audits, which
is the part that convinces people.

Week 9 explains why week 8 does not simply get fixed once you know about it:
the fullness is doing emotional work, and knowing that does not stop it
working.
```

## Module 4 · Resistance and reconstruction

**10** The smart fridge supplies attention — and extracts an itemised record
of the household in exchange.

**11** Degrowth reframes it: not *abolish refrigeration*, but **how much of
this is doing work for us?**

**12** Today: your answer.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:05)

The constructive module, and the set-up for their pitches.

Week 10's finding: the appliance that solves the attention problem solves it
by making the household legible to somebody else. Week 11: the four
antidotes — fermentation, ambient storage, community fridges, shortened
chains — none of them nostalgia, all of them older than the fridge.

Week 11's question is the one every pitch on Wednesday has to answer, so
restate it in exactly those words: **how much of this refrigeration is doing
work for us, and how much is doing work for the system that sold it?**
```

{/* _class: quote */}

> The refrigerator is not a neutral appliance. It is the control centre of
> micropolitics in the modern household, and an illusory fortress against
> temporal decay. By manufacturing expiration anxiety and hoarding illusions,
> it colludes with and sustains late capitalism's logic of food waste and
> overconsumption.

```notes
**~2 min** (0:06)

The thesis in its final form. Read it slowly and completely — it is the only
slide today that earns being read aloud word for word.

Then unpack in one pass, naming where each clause was earned:

- *control centre of micropolitics* — weeks 4, 5, 6.
- *illusory fortress* — week 8. The fortress works; the food really is
  colder. It does not matter, because the binding constraint was never the
  rate of decay. It was **attention**.
- *manufacturing expiration anxiety* — week 7.
- *hoarding illusions* — week 9.
- *colludes with and sustains* — deliberately not "causes." The appliance is
  a component, not a culprit. Students who read it as "the fridge is to
  blame" have the weakest version, and they will meet the objection on the
  next slide.

Say the crucial thing about status: **this is a claim, and you now have the
evidence to argue with it.** In week 1 they could only accept or reject it.
Today they can test it. That difference is the course.
```

# The four strongest objections

## Stated properly, not strawmanned.

```notes
**~30 sec** (0:08)

Signal what this section is for.

You are marking a Critical Essay and forty Final Projects in a fortnight. The
single best predictor of a strong mark is whether the student can state the
case against their own position without flinching. So this section is not
balance for its own sake — it is the assessment criterion, made explicit.

Halfway point. If you are behind, this is the section to compress: put all
four on the board, name them in a sentence each, and move.
```

## 1 · The medical cold chain

Refrigeration has saved an **enormous** number of lives.

Vaccines, insulin, blood, antivenom, transplant organs.

None of that is a door shelf, and conflating them is how this argument gets
dismissed.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:09)

The objection with the highest body count on its side, so lead with it and
concede it fully.

Week 11 already drew the line: a vaccine cold chain and a domestic door shelf
are not the same claim. The de-refrigeration position discredits itself the
moment it stops distinguishing them, and it does so often enough that the
distinction has to be made out loud every time.

What survives the concession: the course's object was always the **domestic**
appliance and the provisioning system around it. That is a real scope
limitation, not a dodge — but say it as a limitation, and hold students to
the same honesty about the scope of their own proposals.
```

---

## 2 · Public health

Reduced foodborne illness. Improved **year-round nutrition**.

Safe milk, safe meat, safe leftovers, in households that would otherwise be
choosing between risk and hunger.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:10)

The objection students most often skip, and the one that most deserves a
hearing.

Refrigeration did not only redistribute food — it made a category of ordinary
illness rarer, and it flattened the seasonal nutrition gap that used to be a
real constraint on health. Both are large, both are well evidenced, and
neither is undone by anything in this course.

The distributional point matters and it cuts against us: the households that
benefit most from reliable refrigeration are poorer households, further from
shops, buying in bulk because bulk is cheaper. Any argument that treats the
fridge as a luxury to be given up has quietly assumed an affluent household.
```

---

## 3 · The category error

Blaming an **appliance** for consumer capitalism may be a category error.

The throughput logic predates the fridge and would survive its removal.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:11)

The sharpest objection intellectually, and the one that a good essay attacks
this course with.

Put it at full strength: if the driver is a mode of provisioning that
requires continuous throughput, then the refrigerator is a *symptom* wearing
the costume of a cause, and a course that spends twelve weeks on the
appliance has mistaken the visible thing for the operative one. Remove every
fridge tomorrow and the logic finds another instrument.

The honest reply is narrower than students expect, so give it in those terms:
the appliance is where the system becomes a **household decision** — the
point at which an abstract logic is executed by a person at 11pm. That makes
it the right site for analysis and for intervention, without making it the
cause. Whether that reply is sufficient is a live question and I do not think
it is fully settled.
```

---

## 4 · Who can afford it

De-refrigeration is a position most easily held by people who can **shop
three times a week**.

Time, money, proximity, and a kitchen you control are all assumed by it.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:12)

The objection with the most immediate consequences for Wednesday, so link it
forward explicitly.

Managing without refrigeration substitutes **household labour and frequent
shopping** for stored cold. Both are unevenly available, and week 2 already
established who absorbs additional domestic work when it appears.

Then land the connection to their pitches, because this is the practical
payoff of the whole slide: this objection is exactly why the two questions
asked of every pitch on Wednesday are *who is this for* and *what does it ask
them to give up*. A proposal that only works for someone with time, money and
a car has answered an easier question than the one asked.
```

## What a defensible cold future would need

- **Honest labels** — what is known, what is not, and what to check
- **Ambient by default** — cold reserved for what genuinely needs it
- **Shared storage** — surplus moving sideways, not downwards into a bin
- **Shorter chains** — less for the cold chain to do
- **Returned competence** — judgement held by households, not rented

None of these requires abolishing anything.

```notes
**~2 min** (0:13)

The constructive close, assembled from weeks 7 and 11 rather than introduced
new. Point at where each came from as you go — honest labels from week 7,
the other four from week 11's antidotes.

The last line is the one to emphasise. Every item is a **reallocation**, not
a removal: less refrigeration doing commercial work, the same or better
refrigeration doing the work that actually protects people. That is the
degrowth move applied to one appliance — a materially smaller system that
meets needs better, with no assumption that less throughput means less
wellbeing.

This slide is deliberately a menu rather than a programme. Their proposals
are the programme, and several will disagree with items on this list, which
is a good outcome and worth saying.
```

## The unplugged picnic

Room-temperature bread. Fruit. Ambient drinks. Last week's ferments.

No cold chain, no fortress, one afternoon.

Not a stunt — the twelve-week argument in **embodied form**.

```notes
**~1 min** (0:16)

Set the picnic up properly, because the framing is what stops it reading as a
gimmick.

It is a demonstration, lasting one afternoon, that a great deal of pleasure
and hospitality requires no fortress at all. It is also — and it is worth
admitting this to the room — the most persuasive thing in the course, which
is slightly humbling for the two of us who spent eleven weeks making the
argument in words.

If the ferments from week 11 are ready, they are the centrepiece and the best
possible object: food that is *better* for having changed, made by this
group, requiring no cold. That is week 1's claim about coexisting with decay,
closing twelve weeks later on a table.
```

## One thing out

Pick a single item. Take it out of the fridge for a week. See what happens.

Butter · tomatoes · bread · onions · unopened condiments

The boundary between "needs cold" and "doesn't" is a **hardened convention**,
not a physical fact.

```notes
**~30 sec** (0:17)

The Unplugged Pledge from the session page, offered as an action rather than
a conclusion. Ten seconds; they can read the rest on the page.

Tomatoes are the one to name aloud if you name any: below about 12 °C the
compounds responsible for flavour stop being produced and the flesh goes
mealy. Refrigeration is close to the worst thing you can do to one. Easiest
pledge on the list to keep, and the most immediately convincing.

Eggs are the standing proof of the convention point — countries disagree
sharply, and the honest answer genuinely depends on where you live and how
they were processed. Do not oversell it.
```
