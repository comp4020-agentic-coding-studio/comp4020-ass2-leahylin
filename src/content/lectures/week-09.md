_Module 3 — The tyranny of time._

A full fridge is a comfortable thing to look at, and this is worth pausing on,
because it is not a rational response to your provisioning situation. You cannot
eat a full fridge before a substantial part of it turns, and at some level you
know that. The fullness is doing something other than feeding you.

This week takes seriously the possibility that the refrigerator's real product is
**reassurance**, and that reassurance is precisely why the waste of week 8 is
tolerated. An empty fridge produces a response out of all proportion to the actual
risk — a low-level existential unease, in households ten minutes from a
supermarket and in no danger of hunger. That reaction is learned, and the
appliance taught it: bulk storage becomes possible, then normal, then the baseline
of what "enough food in the house" feels like. Falling below the baseline
registers as threat rather than as an ordinary Tuesday.

Prepper psychology and pandemic panic buying are the same behaviour with the
volume turned up, which is why they are so instructive. The empty shelves of early
2020 were less a failure of supply than a demonstration of what a great many
households believe a fridge and a freezer are for. Not nutrition. **Control** —
over an unpredictable future, purchased in advance and stored where it can be
seen.

From here the course's economic claim becomes psychological: much consumer
behaviour is the purchase of **emotional value** rather than use value, and the
fridge is among the most efficient emotional-value delivery devices ever installed
in a home.

Inherits from week 8: the waste is structural and measurable. This week asks why
it is comfortable.

## Outline

- fullness as affect rather than provisioning
- the empty fridge as an existential rather than a nutritional problem
- prepper psychology, and the freezer as an object with a theory of the future
- panic buying as ordinary stockpiling under stress
- emotional value versus use value in consumption
