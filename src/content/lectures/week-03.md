_Module 1 — Disenchantment._

The domestic refrigerator is the final two cubic feet of an infrastructure that
spans oceans. Refrigerated shipping, rail, warehousing and air freight together
form the **cold chain**, and your kitchen is its terminal — the last mile, where a
system built for commodity trading delivers into a domestic appliance.

The consequence is that a temperate city eats Chilean cherries in July and
Norwegian salmon on a Tuesday, and does so without anyone experiencing this as
remarkable. Seasonality has not been overcome so much as **abolished as
information**. A cook who can obtain any ingredient in any month no longer needs
to know what grows when, what travels, what to do with a glut, or what a place
tastes like at a particular time of year. That knowledge did not become wrong; it
became unnecessary, which is a more permanent kind of loss.

The ecological accounting is the part that is hardest to see from inside a
kitchen. Refrigerated transport and warehousing are energy-intensive at every
link, the refrigerant gases are themselves potent greenhouse contributors, and the
whole apparatus exists to move perishable goods further than they could otherwise
go. The fridge's promise of abundance has a carbon footprint that is charged to
everyone.

Inherits from week 2: the household's planning work was reorganised around a box
that is always full. This week asks what fills it, and what had to be built.

## Outline

- the cold chain as infrastructure: shipping, rail, warehousing, air freight
- the kitchen as last mile of a commodity system
- seasonality as information, and the cost of abolishing it
- "freshness" as a manufactured category rather than a description
- the ecological ledger: energy, refrigerants and food miles
