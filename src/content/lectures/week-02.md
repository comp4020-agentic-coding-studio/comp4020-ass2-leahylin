_Module 1 — Disenchantment._

The mid-century refrigerator was not sold as a cooling device. It was sold as
**modernity itself**, in white enamel, standing in the corner of a kitchen that
the advertisement had also redesigned. Buy the box and you buy the life around
it: hygiene, abundance, a certain kind of family, and a woman who is smiling
because she has been liberated from work.

Cowan's argument is the one that reorganises the field, and it is empirical
rather than polemical. Household mechanisation did not produce leisure. Standards
rose to consume the savings — food had to be fresher, kitchens cleaner, meals more
varied, waste more carefully managed — and the newly raised standard was enforced
on the same person who had been promised freedom from it.

The refrigerator's specific contribution was to convert **provisioning into
management**. Planning, rotation, inventory, and the continuous judgement of what
is still good became an unpaid, invisible, never-finished managerial job performed
inside the home. The appliance did not take the work. It took the work's
visibility, which is worse, because unseen work cannot be shared or refused.

Inherits from week 1: manufactured cold made storage skill unnecessary. This week
asks what replaced that skill, and on whose time.
