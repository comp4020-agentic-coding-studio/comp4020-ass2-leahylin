**This week's question: what is a refrigerator advertisement actually selling, if
it is not selling refrigeration?**

_Module 1 — Disenchantment._

Inherited from week 1: cold became a purchasable commodity. This week is about how
it was sold, and to whom.

## Before the session

Read the Cowan excerpts.

Then find **two refrigerator advertisements** from between 1950 and 1980 — print
advertisements work best, and archives of mid-century magazine advertising are
easy to search online. Bring them, on a screen or printed. Try to find one from
each end of that range; the change across thirty years is part of the material.

## In the session

Groups of four. Each group works with a pooled set of six to eight
advertisements.

- **First pass — description only** (20 min). No interpretation yet. What is
  literally depicted? Who is in the frame? Where are they standing, what are they
  wearing, what is their posture, who is looking at whom?
- **Second pass — the implicit messages** (35 min). Now interpret, systematically:
  - **Who operates the appliance?** Whose hand is on the door?
  - **Who benefits?** Who is depicted eating, resting, or being served?
  - **Colour and form.** White enamel, chrome, rounded corners. What is white
    doing — purity, hygiene, clinical authority, middle-class arrival?
  - **Scale and placement.** How big is the fridge relative to the person, and
    where is it in the room?
  - **What is promised that is not food?** Status, modernity, marriage, leisure,
    being a good mother.
- **The liberation claim** (25 min). Cowan's argument against the room's findings.
  The advertisements promise freedom from labour; the time-use data says the hours
  did not fall. Where in the advertisement is the work that is about to be
  created rather than removed?
- **Close** (10 min). One sentence per group: what your set was really selling.

## Afterwards

Keep your annotated advertisements. An advertisement is one of the strongest
possible phenomena for the [Critical Essay](/assessments/critical-essay/) — it is
small, dateable, and densely coded, which is exactly what that assessment wants
instead of a broad topic.

Next week the fridge stops being a domestic object and becomes the end of a
supply chain.
