**This week's question: what does a kitchen lose when every ingredient is
available in every month — and what does the availability cost?**

Inherited from week 2: the appliance was sold as modernity. This week asks what
had to be built behind it for the promise to work.

## Before the session

**Photograph the three most off-season or exotic items in your refrigerator.** Not
the three most interesting-looking — the three that have travelled furthest or
that could not have been grown near here this month. Get the label in the shot,
including any country-of-origin marking.

Do this **before** Wednesday. The tutorial is built entirely on your photographs
and there is no substitute set.

Read Freidberg's introduction and your chosen commodity chapter, plus the Twilley
cold chain chapters.

## In the session

Bring a laptop if you have one — one per pair is enough.

- **Building the map** (35 min). Using mapping software, we place every item the
  room brought and trace a plausible route from origin to your kitchen: growing
  region, port or airport, shipping or air freight, distribution centre, retailer,
  your fridge. Where the label does not say, note the gap — an unstateable origin
  is a finding, not a failure.
- **Costing it** (25 min). For each route, estimate the implied ecological cost:
  distance, likely transport mode, refrigeration maintained throughout, and the
  refrigerant itself. **State your uncertainty.** A confident wrong number is
  worse than an honest range, and this discipline is directly assessed in the
  essay.
- **The seasonal calendar** (20 min). We put every item on a calendar of when it
  would be local. Most rooms discover they are collectively eating as though it is
  four different months at once.
- **What was lost** (10 min). If knowing what grows when is no longer necessary,
  is anything lost that a person would actually miss? Argue both sides.
