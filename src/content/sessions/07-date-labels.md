**This week's question: who wrote the date on this jar, what does it legally mean,
and what does obeying it cost you?**

_Module 3 — The tyranny of time._

Welcome back. The register changes here: for six weeks we read the fridge
interpretively, and from now on the argument is measurable.

## Before the session

Bring **5–10 items** with date labels, or clear photographs of them. Prioritise
the confusing ones: labels that say something other than a plain *use by*,
products where the date seems implausible, anything with more than one date on it,
and anything where you have no idea which kind of date it is.

Include the small print. It is usually where the actual meaning is.

Read the Stuart excerpts on date labelling and retail standards.

## In the session

Groups of four, working with the pooled items. This is a forensic exercise — the
labels are the evidence and the regulations are the reference.

- **Classification** (25 min). For every item, decide which of these the date is,
  and record why:
  - a **safety baseline** — a genuine microbiological limit on a high-risk food
  - a **peak of flavour** — a quality prediction, with no safety content
  - **neither** — a date that should never have been printed at all
- **Consulting the regulations** (25 min). Look up what your jurisdiction's food
  safety rules actually require for each product category. The gap between what
  the rules require and what the packet says is the week's central finding, and it
  is usually larger than anybody expects.
- **Drafting an honest label** (30 min). Each group rewrites the labels for three
  items. An honest label has to say what it knows, what it does not know, and what
  the consumer should actually check. Constraint: it must be short enough to
  print and specific enough to act on.
- **Reading them out** (20 min). Which honest labels would a manufacturer accept?
  Which would their lawyer refuse, and on what grounds? That refusal is the
  political economy of the whole week in one sentence.
